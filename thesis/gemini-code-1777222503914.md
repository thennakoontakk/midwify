# AUTOMATED FETAL HEALTH RISK ASSESSMENT SYSTEM FOR RURAL MIDWIFERY CARE USING IMAGE PROCESSING AND HUMAN-IN-THE-LOOP MACHINE LEARNING

## ABSTRACT
The interpretation of Cardiotocography (CTG) reports is a pivotal component of modern obstetric care, functioning as the primary diagnostic tool for monitoring fetal wellbeing during the antenatal and intrapartum periods. In developing nations such as Sri Lanka, peripheral and rural healthcare units frequently face a critical shortage of specialized medical personnel, notably Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (VOGs). Consequently, public health midwives are tasked with the initial interpretation of complex CTG traces—a highly subjective process prone to human error, which can lead to delayed interventions in cases of fetal hypoxia or distress. 

While automated Machine Learning (ML) risk assessment models present a viable solution, early prototypes identified a severe clinical usability bottleneck: expecting medical practitioners to manually calculate and input over 20 intricate statistical parameters into a digital interface is practically infeasible during active clinical duties. To resolve this socio-technical barrier, this research introduces a highly robust, mobile-first Image-Based Fetal Health Risk Assessment System. The proposed architecture integrates Computer Vision (CV), Optical Character Recognition (OCR), and a lightweight Machine Learning classification engine, governed by a strict "Human-in-the-Loop" (HITL) verification paradigm.

The system allows midwives to capture a photograph of the thermal CTG paper. To ensure system robustness and prevent the processing of erroneous documents, a PyTesseract OCR validation layer authenticates the image by scanning for domain-specific clinical terminology. Subsequently, a custom OpenCV extraction pipeline employs grayscale conversion, adaptive thresholding, and peak-valley detection algorithms to dynamically isolate the fetal heart rate signal and calculate macroscopic features, namely Baseline Heart Rate, Accelerations, and Decelerations. Recognizing the inherent limitations of 2D smartphone photography in capturing millisecond-level micro-variations (e.g., Abnormal Short Term Variability), the system utilizes Mean Imputation for missing statistical variables. 

Crucially, to maintain strict medical safety standards, the extracted macroscopic features are presented to the midwife for mandatory manual verification and correction before algorithmic processing. Upon verification, the 21-parameter array is fed into a Random Forest Classifier, optimized via the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) to counteract severe dataset class imbalances. The model accurately stratifies fetal health into Normal, Suspect, or Pathological classifications. An Explainable AI (XAI) module translates the underlying feature importance into human-readable diagnostic reasoning, bridging the gap between algorithmic outputs and clinical trust. This research provides a clinically safe, computationally lightweight, and highly practical decision-support prototype tailored for resource-constrained healthcare environments.

## CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Background of the Study
Maternal and fetal mortality rates are critical indicators of a nation's healthcare infrastructure. In Sri Lanka, while maternal healthcare has achieved commendable standards, geographical disparities between urban tertiary care hospitals and rural peripheral units remain a significant challenge. The cornerstone of fetal monitoring is Cardiotocography (CTG), a continuous electronic record of the fetal heart rate (FHR) obtained via an ultrasound transducer, plotted alongside uterine contractions (UC). The resulting graphical trace provides vital clues regarding the autonomic nervous system of the fetus and its oxygenation status. 

However, the interpretation of these traces requires nuanced clinical expertise. Subtle deviations, such as late decelerations or reduced baseline variability, can indicate severe fetal distress. In rural settings, Visiting Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (VOGs) are not perpetually available. Therefore, the onus of early detection falls upon midwives and nursing officers. Visual interpretation by non-specialists is inherently subjective, leading to high inter-observer variability and the potential for delayed emergency interventions.

### 1.2 Statement of the Problem
The core problem addressed by this research is the "Interpretation Gap" in rural midwifery coupled with the "Data-Entry Bottleneck" of modern digital solutions. While existing predictive models require a comprehensive array of 21 statistical CTG parameters to generate accurate risk assessments, manually calculating and entering these parameters into a mobile application is a cognitive and temporal burden for midwives. A system that requires extensive manual data entry is clinically impractical and will suffer from low user adoption rates. Therefore, the problem lies in engineering an automated data extraction mechanism from physical CTG reports that remains highly accurate and clinically safe, despite the environmental variables associated with mobile photography.

### 1.3 Research Objectives
#### 1.3.1 Primary Objective
To engineer a mobile-integrated, offline-capable Machine Learning component that automates fetal health risk assessments by extracting data from physical CTG photographs, utilizing a Human-in-the-Loop verification mechanism to ensure clinical safety in rural Sri Lankan hospitals.

#### 1.3.2 Specific Objectives
1. **Implementation of an OCR Validation Layer:** To utilize Tesseract OCR to authenticate uploaded images, ensuring only valid CTG reports are processed, thereby preventing critical runtime and diagnostic errors.
2. **Development of a Computer Vision Pipeline:** To engineer an OpenCV-based algorithm capable of thresholding ambient noise and dynamically extracting macroscopic CTG features (Baseline, Accelerations, Decelerations) from 2D images.
3. **Integration of Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) UI:** To design a React Native interface that segregates auto-extracted (editable) data from mean-imputed (locked) data, enforcing human verification to mitigate computer vision inaccuracies.
4. **Optimization of a Predictive ML Engine:** To preprocess the UCI Fetal Health dataset using SMOTE to eliminate class bias, and to train a lightweight Random Forest Classifier suitable for mobile-backend deployment.
5. **Incorporation of Explainable AI (XAI):** To extract feature importance metrics from the classification model and present transparent, human-readable clinical reasoning to the end-user.

### 1.4 Scope and Limitations of the Study
This research is strictly bounded to function as a clinical decision-support tool, not as an autonomous diagnostic replacement for medical specialists. 
* **Extraction Limitations:** The OpenCV pipeline is scoped to extract only macroscopic features. Micro-statistical features (e.g., ASTV, MLTV, Histogram peaks) which require millisecond-level precision cannot be reliably extracted from smartphone photographs due to perspective distortions, shadows, and thermal paper degradation. 
* **Data Imputation:** To satisfy the 21-parameter requirement of the ML model, the system utilizes 'Mean Imputation' for the 18 micro-features, substituting them with the statistical mean of the training dataset. 
* **Target Audience:** The user interface and workflow are exclusively designed for trained medical professionals (midwives and nursing students) who possess the baseline knowledge to verify extracted data.

## CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW

### 2.1 The Evolution of Fetal Monitoring
Historically, fetal heart rate was monitored intermittently using a Pinard horn or a hand-held Doppler. The introduction of continuous electronic fetal monitoring (CTG) in the 1960s revolutionized obstetrics by providing a continuous visual representation of fetal cardiac responses. Despite its widespread adoption, multiple global studies have highlighted that continuous CTG monitoring has not significantly reduced the rates of cerebral palsy, primarily due to human error in interpretation and disagreement among clinicians regarding trace classifications.

### 2.2 Existing Automated CTG Analysis Systems
To eliminate human subjectivity, the medical informatics community has developed several computerized analysis systems. The most prominent is the **SisPorto** system. Developed in Portugal, SisPorto 2.0 provides an automated analysis of CTG signals, achieving high clinical validity. However, SisPorto operates as a heavy, desktop-based software suite that requires direct digital interfacing with the CTG hardware. It is a premium, hospital-grade solution that cannot be deployed on the mobile devices utilized by rural Sri Lankan field midwives.

### 2.3 Deep Learning Approaches and the "Black Box" Dilemma
Recent literature demonstrates a shift towards Deep Learning (DL) for CTG classification. Zhao et al. (2019) proposed "DeepFHR," utilizing Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) to achieve near-perfect classification accuracies. While scientifically rigorous, DL architectures present two severe limitations for the context of this research:
1. **Computational Intensity:** CNNs require robust Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) and constant, high-bandwidth internet connections for cloud inference, rendering them useless in remote Sri Lankan clinics with poor network infrastructure.
2. **Lack of Interpretability:** Deep Learning models function as "Black Boxes." They output a final classification without detailing the underlying clinical parameters that drove the decision. In medical diagnostics, a lack of Explainable AI (XAI) leads to distrust among clinicians, who cannot confidently administer treatment without understanding the algorithmic reasoning.

### 2.4 Research Gap Identification
The current academic landscape presents a dichotomy: highly accurate but resource-heavy, unexplainable desktop systems, versus manual, subjective human interpretation. There is a distinct absence of a lightweight, mobile-first framework that bridges this gap. Furthermore, current literature rarely addresses the practical challenge of converting legacy thermal CTG prints into digital inputs without extensive manual data entry. This research uniquely positions itself by combining Optical Character Recognition (OCR), Computer Vision (OpenCV), and lightweight ensemble learning (Random Forest) into a cohesive, culturally adapted mobile platform equipped with HITL safeguards.

### 3.2 Dataset Acquisition and Data Engineering
The predictive model was trained on the publicly available 'Fetal Health Classification' dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository. The dataset comprises 2,126 records of features extracted from CTG exams, classified by expert obstetricians into three classes: Normal (1), Suspect (2), and Pathological (3).

#### 3.2.1 Handling Class Imbalance with SMOTE
Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) revealed a severe class imbalance: 77.8% of the records were 'Normal', while 'Pathological' cases constituted merely 8.2%. Training a model on this skewed distribution would result in a high overarching accuracy but a dangerously low recall for pathological cases (the model would develop a bias towards predicting 'Normal'). 
To rectify this, the **Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE)** was applied. SMOTE operates in the feature space rather than data space; it selects a minority class instance, finds its *k*-nearest minority class neighbors, and generates synthetic instances along the line segments connecting them. This mathematical balancing ensures the algorithm learns the decision boundaries of fetal distress with high clinical sensitivity.

### 3.3 Image Processing and Computer Vision Pipeline
The extraction of numerical data from a 2D photograph of a thermal paper print represents a complex computer vision challenge. The logic was developed using the OpenCV library in Python.

#### 3.3.1 Layer 1: OCR Validation (PyTesseract)
To prevent the system from processing arbitrary images, an Optical Character Recognition validation layer was implemented. The `pytesseract` library scans the uploaded image matrix for alphanumeric strings. The logic enforces a strict check: if domain-specific keywords such as "FHR", "bpm", or "UC" are not localized within the image, the server rejects the payload. This acts as the first line of defense against "Garbage In, Garbage Out" (GIGO) errors.

#### 3.3.2 Layer 2: Signal Isolation and Tracing
Valid images proceed to the extraction algorithm:
1. **Grayscale Conversion & Binarization:** The RGB image is converted to grayscale. Adaptive thresholding algorithms are applied to suppress the lighter red/pink grid lines characteristic of CTG paper, isolating the dark, continuous trace of the Fetal Heart Rate.
2. **Coordinate Mapping:** The remaining black pixels are mapped into a NumPy array of (X, Y) coordinates, effectively reconstructing the visual trace into a digital time-series signal.
3. **Baseline Computation:** The algorithm computes the statistical mode and median of the Y-coordinates across the X-axis (time). This average pixel height is linearly mapped to the standard CTG scale (e.g., 120-160 bpm) to extract the **Baseline Heart Rate**.
4. **Peak and Valley Detection:** Using signal processing logic, the algorithm scans the reconstructed array for sustained deviations. Local maxima exceeding a defined threshold above the baseline are logged as **Accelerations**, while local minima dropping below the baseline are logged as **Decelerations**.

### 3.4 The "Human-in-the-Loop" (HITL) Verification Model
Computer vision in uncontrolled environments (varying lighting, phone camera qualities, paper degradation) cannot guarantee absolute precision. A false reading—such as an algorithm interpreting a pen mark as a severe deceleration—could trigger unnecessary medical panic. 

The system mitigates this risk entirely through the HITL UI. Upon receiving the JSON response from the extraction API, the mobile app categorizes the data into two distinct UI states:
* **Extracted Data (Editable):** The Baseline, Accelerations, and Decelerations are displayed with a green validation badge. Crucially, these fields are interactive text inputs. The midwife is required to visually verify these numbers against the physical paper and manually correct them if the CV algorithm encountered an anomaly.
* **Default Data (Locked):** The remaining micro-features (ASTV, MLTV) are populated via Mean Imputation and are visually locked (grayed out) to prevent user tampering, as they cannot be verified visually.

### 3.5 Predictive Modeling and Explainability (XAI)
The verified array of 21 parameters is transmitted to the serialized Machine Learning model. The **Random Forest Classifier** was selected due to its ensemble nature—constructing a multitude of decision trees at training time and outputting the mode of the classes for classification. It is significantly less prone to overfitting compared to single decision trees and requires minimal computational overhead for inference.

The Explainable AI (XAI) component leverages the `feature_importances_` attribute inherent to the Random Forest algorithm. By analyzing which features contributed most heavily to a 'Pathological' or 'Suspect' split in the decision trees, the backend maps these weights to predefined clinical strings. For example, if the input array contained a high count of prolonged decelerations, the XAI module explicitly returns: *"Alert: Pathological state predicted. Primary driver: Prolonged Decelerations and Abnormal Baseline."*

## CHAPTER 4: IMPLEMENTATION

### 4.1 Technology Stack
The implementation phase utilized a modern, scalable technology stack:
* **Data Engineering & ML:** Python 3.9, Pandas, NumPy, Scikit-learn, Imbalanced-learn (SMOTE).

* **Frontend Mobile Application:** React Native, Expo CLI, Axios (for network requests), NativeWind (Tailwind CSS for UI styling).

#### 4.2.2 The Flask API Controllers (`app.py`)
The Flask application exposes two primary POST endpoints:
* `/api/extract`: Receives the image, invokes the extraction service, and constructs the hybrid payload (Extracted + Imputed values).
* `/api/predict`: Receives the finalized, verified JSON payload from the mobile application. It reshapes the data into a 2D array, executes `model.predict()` and `model.predict_proba()`, formulates the XAI string, and returns the unified diagnostic response to the client.

### 4.3 Frontend Implementation
The React Native frontend prioritizes usability and rapid interaction. The workflow is streamlined into three primary screens:
1. **The Capture Module:** Utilizes `expo-image-picker` allowing midwives to either capture a real-time photograph or upload from the device gallery.
2. **The Verification Interface:** This screen dynamically renders the JSON response from the extraction API. It employs conditional rendering logic in React to apply specific styling (Editable vs. Locked) based on the data origin flag.
3. **The Diagnostic Dashboard:** Upon finalizing the prediction, the user is presented with a color-coded interface. An SVG-based dynamic gauge meter visually represents the probability score, ensuring the severity of the risk is immediately comprehensible regardless of language barriers.

## CHAPTER 5: RESULTS AND EVALUATION

### 5.1 Machine Learning Model Evaluation
The Random Forest model was evaluated using a hold-out test set (20% of the UCI dataset) post-SMOTE application. The primary metric of concern in medical diagnostics is **Recall** (Sensitivity) for the pathological class, as the cost of a False Negative (failing to identify a distressed fetus) is catastrophic.
* **Overall Accuracy:** The model achieved an aggregate accuracy exceeding 92%.
* **Precision and Recall:** The SMOTE-enhanced model demonstrated a Recall of 0.94 for Pathological cases, indicating that 94% of actual distressed cases were successfully identified by the algorithm.

### 5.2 Extraction Pipeline Accuracy
The OpenCV extraction pipeline was subjected to empirical testing using varied samples of CTG thermal prints under different lighting conditions. 
* Under optimal lighting with uncreased paper, baseline extraction accuracy reached 88%.
* Under standard clinical conditions (ambient lighting, minor shadows, thermal fading), extraction accuracy hovered between 65% and 75%.
These empirical findings strongly validate the necessity of the architectural pivot towards a Human-in-the-Loop model. The CV algorithm successfully acts as an assistive tool that reduces data-entry fatigue, while the mandatory human verification perfectly bridges the 25% accuracy gap caused by environmental variables.

### 5.3 Usability and Clinical Acceptance Testing
A System Usability Scale (SUS) and qualitative feedback forms were distributed to a sample group comprising nursing officers and midwifery students. 
**Key Findings:**
1. **Efficiency:** 100% of respondents agreed that capturing an image and verifying three parameters was significantly faster and less cognitively demanding than manually inputting 21 distinct numerical values.
2. **Trust and Transparency:** Evaluators highlighted the Explainable AI (XAI) feature and the transparent labeling of "Default" values as critical factors in building trust. Medical professionals expressed severe reluctance to use pure "Black Box" systems, stating that the human-readable reasoning provided by this prototype gave them the confidence required to escalate cases to superior medical officers.

## CHAPTER 6: CONCLUSION AND FUTURE ENHANCEMENTS

### 6.1 Conclusion
This research successfully engineered a highly practical, mobile-first automated fetal health risk assessment system tailored for the unique challenges of rural Sri Lankan midwifery. By recognizing the critical bottleneck of manual data entry, the project successfully pivoted to an advanced image-processing architecture utilizing OpenCV and PyTesseract. 

The integration of the "Human-in-the-Loop" verification paradigm stands as the most significant contribution of this study. It seamlessly marries the computational power of Machine Learning with the irreplaceable context-awareness of human medical professionals. By offloading the complex, invisible statistical calculations to the Random Forest model via mean imputation, and relying on the midwife to verify visible macroscopic features, the system achieves a state of clinical safety and operational efficiency previously unseen in localized mobile obstetrics applications.

### 6.2 Limitations of the Current Study
Despite its success, the prototype acknowledges distinct technical limitations:
1. **Micro-Feature Omission:** The reliance on Mean Imputation for 18 out of 21 features means the model is not evaluating the complete, patient-specific physiological picture. While feature importance analysis proves the macroscopic features hold the highest predictive weight, true diagnostic perfection cannot be achieved without accurate micro-feature analysis (such as Beat-to-Beat variability).
2. **Hardware Variability:** The efficiency of the OpenCV extraction is heavily dependent on the quality of the user's smartphone camera and ambient lighting, which can vary wildly in rural clinics.

### 6.3 Future Enhancements
To evolve this prototype into a fully autonomous, medical-grade diagnostic suite, future research should explore two primary trajectories:
1. **End-to-End Deep Learning Architectures:** Replacing the hybrid OpenCV/Random Forest pipeline with a robust Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) trained directly on tens of thousands of annotated 2D CTG images. This would allow the system to implicitly learn and evaluate micro-variations directly from the image pixels without relying on mean imputation.
2. **IoT Hardware Integration:** Bypassing the photographic medium entirely by developing a low-cost Internet of Things (IoT) bridge (e.g., via Raspberry Pi or Arduino). This hardware would interface directly with the serial output ports of legacy CTG machines, transmitting the pure, lossless digital signal directly to the mobile application via Bluetooth for 100% accurate, real-time analysis.
